Producing and receiving radio waves

An alternating current in the transmitting mast makes a wave in the electric and magnetic fields. The wave travels outwards, and when it reaches another aerial it makes an alternating current there too.

1. The alternating current

Radio waves are produced by oscillations in electrical circuits. The AC signal makes the electrons in the mast oscillate up and down, so the current changes direction every half cycle. The electrons only oscillate back and forth; they never leave the metal. Right now the frequency is 100,000 Hz (100 kHz): 100,000 complete cycles every second.

2. The wave travels

The oscillating current produces oscillating electric (E) and magnetic (B) fields. Radio waves are transverse electromagnetic waves: the fields oscillate at right angles to each other and to the direction the wave travels. They travel at the speed of light (3 × 108 m/s) and transfer energy from the transmitter to the receiver. Since wave speed = frequency × wavelength, a higher frequency gives a shorter wavelength.

3. Receiving the wave

When radio waves are absorbed by the receiving aerial, the oscillating electric field makes the electrons in the metal oscillate. This induces an alternating current with the same frequency as the radio wave, but much smaller. The further the receiver is from the transmitter, the weaker the signal.

1. The carrier wave

A microphone turns sound into an electrical signal. A radio wave with a constant amplitude and frequency carries no information, because every cycle is the same: this is the carrier wave. To send the signal, the transmitter changes the carrier wave to match it. This is called modulation.

2. AM and FM

In amplitude modulation (AM), the amplitude of the carrier wave is varied to match the signal, while its frequency stays constant. In frequency modulation (FM), the amplitude stays constant and the frequency is varied to match the signal.

3. Decoding

The radio wave is absorbed by the receiving aerial and induces a small alternating current that carries the same variations. The decoder (demodulator) removes the carrier wave and recovers the original signal, which a loudspeaker turns back into sound. Radio waves travel at the speed of light, so the delay is far too short to hear.

1. A digital signal

Information can be sent as a digital signal: a pattern of 1s and 0s. Here the carrier wave is switched on for a 1 and off for a 0. Morse code works the same way, using short pulses (dots) and long pulses (dashes). SOS is three dots, three dashes, three dots.

2. Noise

Every receiver also picks up random noise. To tell whether the wave is there, the decoder has to look at several complete cycles of the wave. If a pulse lasts less than a few cycles, the decoder cannot tell a 1 from a 0, and short pulses blur together.

3. Higher frequency, more data

A higher-frequency wave fits more cycles into each pulse, so the pulses can be shorter and still be decoded. Shorter pulses mean more 1s and 0s every second: a higher data rate. This is why higher-frequency waves can carry more information. Try it: at a lower frequency, the message only gets through if it is sent more slowly, at a lower data rate.

Did you know? Wi-Fi uses microwaves with a frequency of about 2.4 or 5 billion hertz (GHz), so each pulse can be extremely short. That is how it can send hundreds of millions of 1s and 0s every second.
Did you know? In real life broadcasting the carrier wave has a frequency 100s or 1000s of times greater than the signal. AM stations broadcast at about 0.5 to 1.6 MHz and FM stations at about 100 MHz, while the sound signal changes up to 20,000 times a second (20 kHz).

1. Long and short wavelengths

Long-wave radio has a wavelength of about 1.5 km. FM radio and TV use much shorter wavelengths, of a few metres or less. All of them travel at the same speed, the speed of light, so the shorter the wavelength, the higher the frequency. The wavelengths shown here are not to scale.

2. Bending round the hill

When a wave passes the edge of an obstacle, it spreads out. This is called diffraction. Diffraction is greatest when the wavelength is similar in size to the obstacle, so long-wave radio diffracts over the hill and reaches the valley behind it.

3. In the shadow

Shorter wavelengths diffract very little around a large hill, so they leave a shadow behind it where the signal is very weak. FM and TV need a clear line of sight between transmitter and receiver, so their transmitters are built on high ground. Long-wave signals can be received far away, even behind hills.
