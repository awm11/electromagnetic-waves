Black body radiation

Heat a rock and it gives out radiation in all directions. Let a beam of it through a hole and split it with a prism, and the screen shows how much of each colour, and how much infrared, the rock is emitting.

1. Hotter means more of everything

Every object emits electromagnetic radiation. As it gets hotter, it emits more radiation at every wavelength, and the total power goes up enormously. Below about 500 °C, almost all of it is infrared, which we cannot see but can feel as heat.

2. The colour changes

As the temperature rises, the peak of the emission moves to shorter wavelengths. A hot object first glows dull red, then orange, yellow and white. The very hottest stars look blue-white. Only very hot objects, like the Sun, give out much ultraviolet. The thermometer in the infrared heats up long before the screen shows any light.

3. Reading the curve

The black body curve plots intensity against wavelength. Its height at each wavelength tells you how bright that part of the screen is. The real range of brightness is far too big to show, so the screen is squashed: each step brighter is about ten times more light.

Did you know? In 1800, William Herschel split sunlight with a prism and put thermometers in each colour. The one just beyond the red end, where he could see nothing, got hottest of all. He had discovered infrared. (A real rock would melt long before reaching the temperature of the Sun, so this one is imaginary!)
